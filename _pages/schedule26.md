## Claude

Reflection on Digital Literacy

When I started my Interactive Media degree, I assumed digital literacy meant being fast with software. Two years in, I think it is closer to knowing what to do when the software stops helping. I can move around Figma, Premiere, and Unity without thinking much, but that fluency is narrower than it feels. It mostly covers making things. Understanding where information comes from, how it is stored, and who gets to interpret it is a different skill, and this course keeps pointing at the gap. That gap is where I want to spend my next two years.

For content creation, my habits are fairly solid. I name project files by date, project, and version, I back everything up in two places, and I storyboard on paper or in Figma before I open a timeline. Video and motion work happens in Premiere and After Effects, prototypes in Unity, and quick sketches in Procreate. Socially, most of my collaboration runs through Discord, group chats, and shared Miro boards. I have learned the hard way that a decision made in a voice call does not exist until somebody writes it down. Tone matters as well: a post that works on Instagram can read as flippant in an email to a professor.

On the research side I am less polished. Zotero handles my citations, and I keep notes in Obsidian, linked by topic instead of by course, which has helped me spot connections I would have missed. For data, I am comfortable in Google Sheets and Excel, and I have used Python with pandas in a Jupyter notebook to clean survey responses for a user testing project. I can filter, pivot, and chart. What I do badly is document what I did to the data. If someone asked me to reproduce my cleaning steps from last semester, I would struggle, and that is a real weakness.

The room is a useful mirror. Computer Science students tend to read a spreadsheet as a problem waiting to be scripted. Film and New Media majors notice framing, pacing, and what an interface makes emotionally easy or hard. Engineering students ask about tolerances and failure cases. Environmental Studies brings datasets that are messy, geographic, and political all at once, and the Literature and Creative Writing students are often the ones asking what nobody else thought to ask: whose voice is missing from this archive? None of us has the whole toolkit, and I am starting to think that is the point.

Digital humanities changes how I look at my own field. Tools like Voyant for text analysis or QGIS for mapping make it possible to read a thousand game reviews or a decade of climate reporting at a scale one person cannot manage, while still needing a human to decide what the patterns mean. It makes interactive media something to study as well as something to build. Collaboration is where my computational thinking gets tested. Breaking a big question into parts, spotting repeated patterns, and deciding which details to ignore are things I do by instinct in design, but I have never had to explain them to a coder and a poet in the same conversation. Doing that forces me to be precise.

AI is the area where I feel most unsettled and most curious. I use language models to debug code, brainstorm, and summarize dense readings, and I have caught them inventing citations with total confidence. That taught me to treat output as a lead instead of a source. I want to understand how training data shapes what a model treats as normal, whose work went into it, and how bias ends up inside systems that look neutral. Reading about datasets as cultural artifacts, not raw facts, has already changed how I see them.

My honest assessment is that I am strong on production and weaker on reproducibility, statistics, and critique. I trust a clean chart more than I should. What this course is teaching me is that a visualization is an argument, and every choice about scale, color, and exclusion is part of it. I would like to learn version control with Git, get more comfortable writing R or Python from scratch, and practice reading a dataset skeptically before I analyze it. Most of all, I want to keep working in mixed teams. The most useful thing I have figured out so far is that I do not need to become everyone in the room. I need to learn enough of their language to build something together.

This interdisciplinary mix illustrates why the Digital Humanities offer such compelling new horizons for my studies. Interactive Media often focuses on the "how" of tech—building interfaces, user experience, and computational logic. Digital Humanities forces me to ask the "why." Collaborating across these disciplines significantly expands my approach to computational thinking. This perspective is especially vital as we navigate the world of Artificial Intelligence. Machine learning models, predictive algorithms, and generative tools require us to develop a critical AI literacy—one that moves beyond mere prompt engineering to inspect training datasets, algorithmic fairness, and ethical implications. Rather than viewing AI as a black-box generator, working collaboratively helps me treat it as an interactive system to be dissected, audited, and co-created with.

Critically reflecting on my current abilities, I recognize that while I possess strong technical fluency in front-end prototyping, creative coding, and media production, my analytical framework often leans heavily toward aesthetic and functional design. In this course, I am discovering that true digital literacy requires equal parts technical fluency and critical skepticism. I am learning to interrogate the infrastructure underlying the tools I take for granted—the data pipelines, platform economies, and spatial biases embedded within software interfaces.

Moving forward, I want to explore new ways of thinking that bridge critical theory with computational practice. I aim to delve deeper into ethics-first design, spatial computing, and human-AI interaction frameworks. By integrating the narrative depth of the humanities with the systematic rigor of computer science and environmental modeling, I hope to develop a digital practice that is not only technologically inventive, but socially conscious and human-centered.

# The Final Response

## 1. Transferable skills

- **Data wrangling:** Much of healthcare data is unstructured: clinical notes, scanned records, faxed referrals, PDFs. Turning an OCR'd newspaper archive into clean, structured rows is the same core problem as extracting fields from discharge summaries.
- **Pattern recognition:** Clustering images with CLIP or Orange is conceptually close to medical image triage. Corpus analysis maps to NLP on clinical notes, patient feedback, and adverse event reports.
- **AI-assisted extraction:** You learn where LLMs drop details or hallucinate structure. In health, that instinct becomes validation pipelines and human-in-the-loop design, which is a real product differentiator.
- **Visualization:** Dashboards for clinicians or administrators, and communicating uncertainty to non-technical people.
- **Geospatial analysis:** Access-to-care mapping, clinic placement, and disease surveillance.

## 2. Ways of thinking

- **Analytical:** Interrogating provenance ("who made this archive, and what did they leave out?") becomes "whose data trained this model?"
- **Quantitative:** Clusters and topic models depend on the parameters you choose. Healthcare needs people who treat model output as a claim to test, not a fact.
- **Ethical:** Archives encode the biases of whoever kept them. Health data does too. A well-known example is a widely used risk algorithm that used healthcare spending as a proxy for need and systematically underestimated illness in Black patients (Obermeyer et al., 2019). Health equity work is largely this kind of critique applied to data.
- **Systems:** You see the whole pipeline from source to structure to interpretation. In health, that is intake, record, billing, analytics, and decision, where errors compound at each handoff.

## 3. Non-obvious connections

- **Absence is data.** Gaps in a newspaper archive reveal who was covered and who wasn't. In health data, missingness is informative: who skips follow-ups, which tests never get ordered, which communities are absent from trials.
- **Classification as power.** Archival taxonomies reflect the assumptions of their creators. So do diagnostic codes like ICD, and they shape reimbursement, research, and what counts as a "real" condition.
- **OCR errors resemble ambient scribe errors.** Handling degraded, noisy text is the same challenge as transcribing clinical conversations, especially with accents, dialects, or code-switching. Arabic and dialectal clinical NLP is underserved, which is a genuine startup gap.
- **Close vs. distant reading.** Humanists toggle between one text and a whole corpus. Medicine has the same tension between the individual patient and the population signal, and good health-tech products support both.
- **Patient narratives as evidence.** Text mining methods can analyze free-text patient stories, reviews, or forum posts to surface experiences that structured fields miss, which is the territory of patient-reported outcomes and narrative medicine.

## How to use this

Frame your assignments as portfolio pieces. For example, write up your newspaper-archive pipeline as "converting unstructured historical records into structured, auditable data, with documented error rates and bias checks." That language speaks directly to health-tech founders and hiring managers.
